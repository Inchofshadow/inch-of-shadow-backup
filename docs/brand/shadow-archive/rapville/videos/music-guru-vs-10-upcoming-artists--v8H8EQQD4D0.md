# Music Guru Vs 10 Upcoming Artists
URL: https://youtu.be/v8H8EQQD4D0 | Length: 49:39 | Views: 365 | Date: not in header
Format: a debate. A RAPVILLE representative (apparently the co-founder "Junior"; the caption at 34:25 is garbled) faces a series of sceptical artists. Much of it defends their service, but several frameworks come out along the way.

## Core idea
He argues that going viral in 2026 is still possible if you post intentionally (hooks, a world) rather than "song out now" videos. Content beats ads for building a remembered brand. Formats should be copied and remixed to fit the song's emotion. Views are only the first of three layers (views -> virality -> community/income), and long-term world-building is the goal. The music quality caps conversion.

## Lessons and tactics
1. (00:59) "It's too saturated" rebuttal: accounts with 500-5,000 followers still go viral. The fix is knowing what to post.
2. (01:21) Most artists post **"song out now" videos** instead of thinking how to build a world and hook attention. Post intentionally to reach the right audience.
3. (01:57) Reach the masses to raise the chance of reaching your exact audience (Super Bowl analogy). Then the next stage is community.
4. (02:21) RAPVILLE's own lesson: they first sold virality, then realised artists want streams and to go full-time, not to be "songfluencer creators". So they worked out the **funnel**: content that converts, i.e. **bottom-of-funnel and middle-of-funnel content**.
5. (03:05) Music quality affects viewer-to-stream conversion. Claimed: "90% of artists get less than 500 views on every video."
6. (03:41) Input/output: if the same kind of video gets no views, change what you post.
7. (04:10) Data-volume argument: about 180 clients posting daily ≈ 3,000+ videos a month (his maths in the caption is muddled), so they learn in a day what a single artist learns in a month.
8. (05:02) Two ways to pay: **time or money**. If no urgency, figure it out yourself (he says it may take years).
9. (05:35) Their process starts with a **one-hour branding session** of questions. Many artists don't know what they stand for, so the brand is built around the songs they already made (the most authentic thing) and checked against the music. Music + audience + authority/authenticity aligned = artist brand, then amplify it.
10. (10:44) **Ads vs content: "choose your guarantee"**. Ads guarantee spend and results capped by budget ("the more you pay, the more you can play"). Content is riskier but uncapped if it goes viral.
11. (11:44) Content builds trust; ad viewers are cold and swipe past. People who choose to keep watching, follow and view stories have more intent to become true fans.
12. (12:28) Building from zero, the priority is that people **remember who you are**. Ads buy one small interaction (like, follow, stream).
13. (12:53) In 2026 targeting is Meta's AI job, so **the content is the only differentiator** in ad performance. Running ads before organic content means paying for an experiment you could run organically. **Run ads on your best-performing organic video** for the cheapest, best-converting ad.
14. (13:24) People go viral because of entertainment, controversy, and positioning that makes people want to be part of the world.
15. (14:23) Answer to "it's cringe": look at the comments ("marketing genius", "caught me off guard"). He calls the TV transition "pretty gimmicky" but says it converts. Claimed one video: 10M views -> 10,000 streams in two days.
16. (15:09) Billboards, radio and interviews used to be the attention game, and now it's a different medium (Michael Jackson as an attention-grabber).
17. (15:34) "Cringe is not a content style." Tasteful or cinematic artists don't need gimmicky styles, but they still need a **hook factor** and entertainment. Just sitting with a mic singing in front of a nice view is "selfish". Ask what you are giving the audience. Videos that look nice but have no hook are the common mistake.
18. (16:38) A bespoke, invisible hook is the goal. Process: listen to the lyrics, ask the artist who they are (branding session), then make it congruent with known formats and why they go viral. Ask "what in the first three seconds grabs attention?" and remix that for the track.
19. (16:57) Example analysis: Citizen's ladder fall (Snapchat start -> catch -> cinematic). Ask what made people stay (the shocking fall), then use that same emotion in a different style that transitions into an on-brand cinematic video.
20. (17:16) The audience side sees everything as novel. You see TV transitions everywhere because you're on the marketing side; a rock audience doesn't see what's viral in rap this week. So test cross-niche formats.
21. (17:54) Why copied transitions flop: **trend-chasing copycats** don't know why the original went viral (same TV, same train behind, a song about something else). Reimagine the video but keep the principles, because your audience and song meaning differ.
22. (18:28) Think of content like a **movie**. A sad scene scored with trap or house music wouldn't fit, so the content has to be remixed correctly to your song.
23. (18:55) Citizen's "back and forth" / "BTS vs shot" effect: the strategy (made with Scott) was to make Citizen look cool because the song makes you feel cool. Branding question: **how does my song make people feel?** Then tie it to life moments when people feel that way (e.g. top-down driving in Miami, a "GTA 5/GTA 6 effect"). Listeners going to Spotify want the same emotion shown in the visuals: "visual storytelling".
24. (20:30) Counter-examples (a kid going viral just singing) are explained by **novelty** plus high-quality audio. There are "layers" of content types, and gimmicky formats aren't required.
25. (21:21) Michael Bibi (as captioned): dark shades, bopping to the crowd. He refused to post the TV-transition videos they shot. They say they still got him 5M views on three videos in two weeks by amplifying his authentic brand (claimed).
26. (22:03) (Artist's point, accepted) Fun songs suit humour formats; deep, serious, introspective songs (e.g. a sad Mother's Day song) don't suit a Snapchat-style transition.
27. (24:32) (Artist's point) **Narrative contrast** made several artists blow up: Pete and Baz (older men on drill), "Shabuzzi" (unclear), Post Malone. The RAPVILLE rep's reply (25:13): early on, stay in one specific musical space to build reputation; a genre switch changes your audience, content and fashion.
28. (26:11) Find what makes you unique *within* your audience in two ways: what's genuinely authentic to you, and what music you make (the foundation). Generic music makes a mainstream-size audience hard; a niche community that shares your beliefs is the alternative.
29. (26:46) **"What's your matcha?"** (captioned "matcher"): the identifying device a community uses to signal "you're part of my space" (like "matcha girls" posting matcha on stories). Find the thing you can represent so the community knows you're one of them (Pete and Baz wear chains).
30. (27:52) **Three layers** of an artist's game: Layer 1 = ~200 views per video, nobody sees you. Layer 2 = people see your content, you go viral. Layer 3 = real community, streams, getting paid. You must go through them in order, and Layer 2 means views from the *right* people.
31. (29:18) Views pay indirectly through brand deals, streams and a full-time team (Citizen claimed full-time, pays a team, girlfriend paid as his actress).
32. (30:21) Next step after virality (Citizen, by their account): put **personality** in the videos. That is **middle-of-funnel / trust-level** content, consistent branding so people remember your name, which leads to ticket sales. Connor Price is given as the more-personality example (now shows family in content, does world tours), after an earlier high-view phase ("Spin the Globe").
33. (32:06) Play the **long game**, not quick wins: build community and trust, post consistently, build a world around yourself. Fans "can smell it" when it's inauthentic and you just want their money. (Love the running, not the destination.)
34. (32:53) His own business lesson: early on they spammed stories selling services (quick wins). Switching to trust-building content (videos like this, genuinely helping) worked better.
35. (33:35) If a shoot fails (wrong shot or lighting) and they know it won't go viral, they reshoot.
36. (35:28) **Branding-session questions** (they say they don't tell artists who they are):
    - Imagine your song in a movie: what scene do you see on screen? Where is it?
    - Where does your audience listen to the song most?
    - How do they feel before listening, and after?
    - Imagine you're on stage looking at your audience bopping to the song (rest of the question cut off; unclear).
    These put you "in the lens of your audience".
37. (36:16) **The core message is already in your songs**, in their feelings and lyrics. E.g. an introspective love or breakup song means you're likely an introspective, sad artist who should connect with that side.
38. (37:18) (Artist's point) If you're truly comfortable with your art you won't be perceived as cringe; the moment you think you're cringe, everyone will.
39. (37:49) **Copy and remix framework**: claimed 280,000 songs released daily on Spotify, and creativity is shaped by shared experiences. Novelty for its own sake "feeds the ego". Take strategies that worked in a different niche and remix them for your song and brand.
40. (39:07) Their stated guarantee: within 90 days, at least **5x total monthly views on TikTok + Instagram** or a full refund. They say views aren't the goal.
41. (40:08) (Artist's point, partly agreed) Marketing can fail because the music doesn't hit. **Good music ≠ viral social music**: a TikTok-viral song "needs to be repetitive", catchy, with a relatable quote/line. Not all of Michael Jackson's music would work on social.
42. (41:23) The musical aspect is the core and visuals amplify it. With weaker music there may be a ceiling on reach, so it's better to **own a core audience** than chase mainstream (Drake-level) reach.
43. (41:51) (Artist's point) Hook-heavy "content king" types may be a viral trend rather than a lasting brand. Artists like "Ini" and "Michaela Twig" (names as captioned, unclear) recycle music-video footage without hooks and still connect, because "if the music is not there, you will never last". April 27 (as captioned): claimed 52M views on a first release with barely any content. The rep: outliers exist.
44. (44:38) They say they turn down badly mixed/mastered, off-key music.
45. (45:45) On AI: AI video isn't good enough yet. Your personality is hard to explain to a bot. "The only separator now is their audience." A cheaper videographer or AI doesn't produce results without understanding the "psychology of content".
46. (47:25) Team roles he lists: strategist, creative director, producer (plans and coordinates the shoot, **chooses locations based on the song's emotions**), editor (creates your own editing style, lyric style and effects "you can be known for").
47. (48:18) On AI music: people won't pay to see an AI artist live. "An artist isn't a singer... it's the world they build". It's the feeling of being part of a gang that thinks the same way (Billie Eilish's fans connect with her darker thoughts and want to feel like her best friends).

## Examples he uses
- Adri / Audri (Italian jazz rapper): claimed 200 followers at start, now full-time, touring Italy.
- Eric Bellinger: claimed 30M views on one video, first video ever over 1M, "30x his lifetime of views".
- TV transition video: claimed 10M views -> 10,000 streams in two days.
- Michael Bibi: claimed 5M views on three videos in two weeks, without gimmick formats.
- Citizen: claimed 200M views and ~5M streams average, one song 20M streams; full-time team.
- Connor Price (personality, world tours), Pete and Baz, Post Malone (contrast), April 27 (claimed 52M views, first release), Billie Eilish (world/community), Spotify claim of 280k songs/day, client count claim ~180, package price "up to 8K" (one line says "30k").

## Process / checklists
- Branding-session question list (item 36).
- Format remix steps: find what in the first 3 s grabbed attention -> identify the emotion that made people stay -> re-express it in a style that fits your song -> transition into an on-brand cinematic performance.
- Ads: organic first, then put ad spend behind the best organic performer.
