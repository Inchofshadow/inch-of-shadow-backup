# How The Music Industry Hacks Your Mind
URL: https://youtu.be/f5W-Pj6k4no | Length: 16:44 | Views: 460 | Date: not in header

## Core idea
Five psychological levers that he says make songs spread: social proof, repetition/familiarity, open loops (Zeigarnik), social currency, and scarcity. Each is illustrated with famous examples. His conclusion is that marketing is built into the release itself rather than added after it, and none of the levers needs a label.

## Lessons and tactics
1. (00:00) Hook example: Drake's giant ice block hiding an album release date. He says it got days of global coverage for what could have been a 10-second caption. "That was not a stunt. That was psychology."
2. **Lever 1: Social proof** (01:04)
   - (01:06) People judge content by what others did with it (zero-like reels get swiped, million-like reels pull you in). Music is judged the same way.
   - (01:25) Experimental music platform study (14,000 people, 48 unknown songs; half saw download counts). With counts visible, early downloads snowballed and a different song won on each run. Without counts, the best songs rose every time. (He says "2026" and "researchers from Colombia" as captioned.)
   - (02:19) Joshua Bell busking incognito in a DC metro (Washington Post, 2007): 7 people stopped, about $32. The only missing thing was the crowd signal.
   - (03:04) Kate Bush "Running Up That Hill" (1985) charting after Stranger Things in 2022: the song didn't change, visibility of others caring did.
   - (03:40) Banksy's Central Park stall (2013): signed originals at $60, about $420 for the day.
   - (04:23) Nathan Evans "Wellerman" (Dec 2020): TikTok duets made the growing crowd visible, which caused a snowball.
   - (05:17) Takeaway: we check what others do first and mistake that for our own taste.
   - (05:30) **Tactic**: the first 48 hours of a release are not about reach but about making whatever audience you have *visible*: comments, replies, stitches, anything showing a crowd forming. "A crowd that can be seen is a crowd that grows."
3. **Lever 2: Repetition / familiarity (mere exposure)** (05:48)
   - (06:00) Zajonc 1968 (captioned "Zajunk"): the more often people saw meaningless stimuli, the more they liked them. McGill brain scans: dopamine when the brain correctly predicts what comes next.
   - (06:41) Olivia Rodrigo "drivers license" (Jan 2021) released to Spotify and TikTok at the same time. A simple, emotionally specific hook got recreated (versions, reactions, lip-syncs); people heard the same 8 bars 8-12 times before the official track.
   - (07:22) Labels' conclusion (also "sped up" versions): remixes, covers and reactions aren't the product, the repetition is.
   - (07:35) Citizen's "back and forth" trend (April 2026): he made 5-10 pieces of content, thousands recreated it, and fans heard the song again via the remixes. He credits this for "Time" being his best-performing song.
   - (08:01) **Tactic**: people don't need the full song. They need to hear the same hook 10-15 times over weeks so that the song feels familiar, which drives replays and playlist adds.
4. **Lever 3: Open loops (Zeigarnik effect)** (08:19)
   - (08:26) An unreleased 15-second snippet with no date makes you hunt for it.
   - (08:44) Zeigarnik's Vienna café waiters (captioned "Bloomer Zyenic"): they remember unpaid orders and forget completed ones. Her interrupted-task experiments: the caption's figure ("completed roughly half as much") is garbled, unclear. Point made: the brain keeps returning to open loops until they close.
   - (09:32) Michael Jackson, 1988 Grammys: stood silent at the mic, holding the loop; the anticipation was the performance.
   - (09:57) Drake's ice block = a city-scale open loop; fans extracted the answer within 24 hours.
   - (10:24) Steve Lacy "Bad Habit" (summer 2022): snippet weeks before release; fans predicted lyrics in comments and hunted for live versions; release day became a celebration.
   - (10:57) **Open-loop content types**: a snippet with no release date; a BTS clip that cuts before the payoff; a process clip that never transitions to the final shot; a transition that builds tension and releases it when the performance starts.
   - (11:19) **Tactic**: stop treating release day as day one. The song should already be a question people ask before it exists.
5. **Lever 4: Social currency (why people share)** (11:27)
   - (11:54) Jonah Berger (Wharton): people mostly share things that make them look good, signalling taste, status and being in the know. Neuroscience: the medial prefrontal cortex processes the self.
   - (12:25) Spotify Wrapped = "a personality post wearing a playlist".
   - (12:43) Taylor Swift's Easter eggs (videos, photos, dresses, nails): fans post theories, decode threads and reaction breakdowns as public proof of being a real fan; spreading is how fans prove status.
   - (13:19) "People do not share songs. They share themselves." (paraphrase)
   - (13:29) **Tactic**: ask what sharing this song lets people say about themselves, not how to promote it, and build that answer in before the next post.
6. **Lever 5: Scarcity (+ wanted by others)** (13:41)
   - (13:44) Fans value a bad-quality leaked livestream rip over the polished official release.
   - (14:15) 1975 cookie-jar study: cookies from the near-empty jar (2 vs 10) were rated more desirable, most of all when the jar was emptied by other people. Scarce plus wanted by others "manufactures" value.
   - (14:48) Playboi Carti's leaked "shadow catalog" traded in private Discords. Some leaks outplay official releases, and knowing the unreleased catalog is a rank in the fandom.
   - (15:33) Smaller artists put unreleased music behind **Discord tiers**: getting in is the product, and members recruit because being inside is the flex.
   - (15:54) **Tactic**: give real fans something the public can't have: unreleased tracks, early access, in-person listening parties. They do the selling because selling you sells their taste.
7. (16:19) Wrap-up: marketing is built into the release, not after it. These need no label, only understanding of how the mind works. Artists who make it are better at creating the conditions for spread, not better musicians.

## Process / checklists
- Release-day checklist implied by the levers: (a) build open loops before release (dateless snippet, cut-off BTS, unfinished process clip, tension transitions); (b) repeat the same hook 10-15 times across weeks; (c) in the first 48h make the crowd visible (comments, replies, stitches); (d) decide what sharing the song says about the sharer; (e) give core fans scarce access (unreleased, early access, listening parties, Discord tiers).

## Sales pitch / filler
None explicit in this video. The opening hook and the closing "uncomfortable question" (16:10) are framing only.
