# How to Get ANYONE to Stream Your Music
URL: https://youtu.be/y1Hu1xpf-x8 · Length 19:49 · Views 298 · Speaker: Nile

## Core idea
Four groups can be turned into streamers: strangers, followers who never press play, one-time listeners, and competitors' fans. Strangers convert through feeling, cultural targeting and a "shotgun" format-testing system; followers through a two-week conversion machine (trust-building content, comment automation, story psychology, community management); one-time listeners only through song quality; competitors' fans through copy-and-remix and strategic features.

## Lessons and tactics
GROUP 1: STRANGERS
1. Strangers already have their artists; to stream they must get through "five steps of hassle": stop scrolling, switch platforms, type your name, press play, not get bored during the intro. Only feeling something pushes them through (00:46-01:16).
2. Nobody hears a song neutrally; they hear it through their current state. Make them laugh then play a sad song and it's dead; make them feel alone and the song hits 10x harder; open a feeling (nostalgia, breakup wound) the song resolves (01:16-01:35).
3. Way 1: match the song to the visual with a storyline that fits the feeling, while keeping curiosity, tension and a hook so it can still go viral (01:37-01:51).
4. When a scene works, make variations (same concept, different angle) so viewers have a world to binge; more touch points = higher chance of streaming (02:05-02:17).
5. If the song has no clear emotion, work out what it is selling: a feeling or a claim; e.g. a bar-for-bar rap visualised with a "switching focus" effect and ping-pong back-and-forth; "the skill of the format sells the skill in the rap" (02:19-02:54).
6. Way 2, "cultural targeting": match the content to the specific group the song is for (city, identity, nationality, heritage) (02:56-03:05).
7. Cultural targeting variants: film in the city among the target audience (an "NYC anthem"); use culturally beloved imagery (Vettriano paintings for Italians, slideshow posts); or a single keyword in the caption ("This is why you don't piss off a Hungarian" on a Snapchat-hook transition) (03:05-03:57).
8. People stop for content about them, their city, identity and culture; it stops being an ad from an independent artist (03:59-04:14).
9. "The shotgun method" vs machine-gun posting: don't spray random ideas; test one format (one concept, one piece of psychology) with several spins, e.g. transition format as slap transition, full transition, AI transition = three tests under one umbrella; when one works usually the others do too, giving something to double down on (04:26-05:16).
10. Self-test 1: mute your last five videos; could a stranger guess what the song is about, and be right? (05:19-05:28).
11. Self-test 2: look at 90 days of posts for patterns in which types perform; patterns are data to double down on (05:28-05:40).
12. Branding is not a logo, bio or page aesthetic; it's a stranger feeling a video was made for them, their identity or situation (05:40-05:53).
13. Exercise: write one sentence, "A stranger who watches my video should feel ___", and make the next content idea match it (06:16-06:24).
GROUP 2: FOLLOWERS WHO NEVER STREAM
14. You have about a 2-week window to cement a new follower as a streaming fan before they go cold; design a machine for it (06:42-07:07).
15. Keep the same quality and format that pulled the follower in; double down on it (07:22-07:41).
16. Add "trust building content" based on "the bandwagon effect": people judge what's good by what others already watch (07:42-08:08).
17. Trust content 1, "reaction format": someone (mom, crush, sister) falling in love with the song in real time; engineer every second: early doubt (do they like it?), payoff (they love it); their reaction should steer the viewer's (08:10-08:36).
18. Variation, "listening party": a crowd hyping the artist creates social proof and FOMO; people in the video should look like your target audience (08:36-08:50).
19. "Personality content" (not vlogging breakfast or GRWM): get people to buy into the artist brand, which is also the base for selling tickets. Match it to your brand: the "sad boy" voice-noting his mom in the rain before the sad song; the entertainer making a recognisable series/episodes instead of one-off skits (08:52-09:30).
20. Address new followers directly to camera ("a lot of new people here", "lots of DMs asking for the song"); viewers infer you're popping off (bandwagon framing) (09:30-09:50).
21. Conversion = reduce friction, physical and psychological (10:06-10:17).
22. Physical: "comment automation": every song-promo video has a CTA "comment X and I'll send you the song"; send it immediately, no email or follow required; add only a follow-up one day later with a non-pushy question ("how would you rate it out of 10?", "did it make your playlist yet?") (10:18-10:57). Tool named: ManyChat; set up once (11:01-11:10).
23. Automation has a ceiling; for people without the want, create it psychologically, best on Stories, which only reach your warmest audience (11:12-11:41).
24. Core story format: "growth milestone": screenshot of a milestone (followers, streams) + thank-you; not salesy (11:50-12:07).
25. Showing growth makes viewers buy into the journey: a "cult effect", "the artist I liked before he blew up" (12:08-12:28).
26. Keep doing stories even with a dozen viewers; you're building the habit and the machine (12:30-12:42).
27. Do the unscalable work; word of mouth from a small real fan base compounds whether or not you go viral (12:43-13:10).
28. Other story tactics: repost nice comments/DMs; use polls (e.g. let followers choose the cover art, which brings them into the creative process); a "close friends" story for those who comment, DM and like most, to create exclusivity (13:12-13:43).
29. One-to-one: community management (comments and DMs). Leaving comments empty to seem mysterious is the biggest mistake early on (13:47-14:05).
30. Comment play: reply to "release this" comments with the song name and date; on release, reply again to the same comments "it's out now"; first 48 hours on Spotify decide the campaign (14:08-14:42).
31. Conversion tactics are useless alone, work together (14:44-14:53).
GROUP 3: ONE-TIME LISTENERS
32. No strategy fixes them: marketing gets the first play, song quality gets the second; agencies can make sure the right people hear it, not that they love it (15:03-15:40).
GROUP 4: COMPETITORS' FANS
33. Only after cracking the earlier groups; "fans aren't property" (16:06-16:26).
34. Never copy blatantly (you end up second behind who you copy; audiences "can smell it"); copy and remix for your brand instead (16:29-17:12).
35. Remix can be tiny: on a listening-party format, adding a small zoom-out at the end to keep retention (17:12-17:28).
36. "Strategic features play": joining forces is the fastest way; only works if both sides bring an audience; choose someone with a similar brand at your current stage, not the biggest name (17:40-18:20).
37. Level up: feature with someone in a different market to open it (18:20-18:56), only once you dominate your current field.
38. Your audience is a negotiating position for features, labels, agencies (19:02-19:13).
39. Content compounds unlike ads/playlists; don't expect every post to perform; have a system you stick to regardless of results; you can't get lucky if you quit (19:24-19:47).

## Process / checklists
- Four groups: strangers -> followers -> one-time listeners -> competitors' fans.
- Two-week follower machine: same winning format + trust content (reaction, listening party, personality, direct address) + comment automation (instant send + day-1 follow-up) + stories (milestones, comments/DMs, polls, close friends) + comment/DM replies (date, then "out now").
- Tests: mute-your-last-5 test; 90-day pattern review; "a stranger should feel ___" sentence.
