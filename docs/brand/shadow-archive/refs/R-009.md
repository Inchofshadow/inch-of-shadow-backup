- **The opposite of 0010x0010 (R-008).** Most of his reels have 0 to 3 cuts. One famous moment is held for 7 to 20 seconds and the film's own music or dialogue carries it. He does not edit; he picks.
- **The format that hit hardest: the vertical triptych.** A widescreen film becomes a vertical reel by stacking three horizontal strips, one above the other.
  - In the Three Colours reel each strip is one film of the trilogy, so the colour does all the work: blue, then white, then red, top to bottom. It is only 7 seconds long and got 41.9M views.
  - In The Good, the Bad and the Ugly the three strips are the three gunmen's faces, then their three hands, before the wide shot. The stand-off is shown as a split screen.
  
  This is the clearest way to fit cinema's wide frame into 9:16 without cropping it to death.
- **Other ways he fills 9:16 with a wide film:**
  - letterboxed in the middle on black, with one line of plain white text above ("One of the coolest opening credits ever!", "Rutger Hauer improvised this iconic scene");
  - a square crop with the film's own subtitle;
  - a vertical crop of a close-up (the garlic).
- **The hook is the moment itself, or one line of trivia.** No talking, no title card, no logo. The caption carries the context and a question ("What are your thoughts...?") to pull comments.
- **Colour as the subject.** His biggest reel is a colour idea, not a story. A carousel "Color in filmmaking" in the same 100 does the same.

## What this teaches us (Claude's note, not a decision)

- **One moment, held.** A single strong image with its own sound, no edit, can outperform any cut. It is the counterweight to the 0010x0010 flicker: both are one idea, carried all the way.
- **The vertical triptych** is a ready layout for a reel or a Canvas made from horizontal footage: three strips, three moments, or one colour each.
- **One line of plain text** above a letterboxed frame is enough. It sits outside the picture, never on a face.
- **Films for references:** the list above is the pool. Amir picks which films belong to his world; FILMGRAB (toolkit) then gives each picked film's stills.
