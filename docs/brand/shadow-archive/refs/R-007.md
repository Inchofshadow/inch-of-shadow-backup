## The obscure aesthetics series, and other named looks

Each is a name to search, with what defines it, as he explains it:

- **Great Schema** (part 1). The highest rank of Eastern Orthodox monks: black robe with white embroidery of skulls and scripture, a "wearable memento mori". He notes the design has not changed in centuries ("zero design drift") and that Demna (Balenciaga) and Rick Owens borrowed from monastic dress.
- **Darkest aesthetics, speed run:**
  - Mamuthones: a Sardinian carnival with black wooden masks, sheepskin and heavy cowbells, to chase evil away with noise;
  - the Great Schema;
  - blackletter (and metal logos keeping it alive);
  - the plague doctor's beak mask;
  - Vanitas still lifes (a skull hidden among riches).
- **Tu Kool (土酷)** (Chinese). A 2010s kitsch movement traced to an influencer known as Princess Butterfly. It takes rural Chinese graphics thought tasteless (temples, waterfalls, lotus, proverb posters) and mixes them with glossy early-internet textures and rainbow text, as pride rather than irony.
- **Chinese Dreamcore.** He points to @toorichcity_hhs.
- **Divine Machinery** (episode 6). Religious imagery rebuilt from technology: circuit-board crosses, code as scripture, neon cathedrals, glitching TVs. It spread on Tumblr.
- **Pakistani truck art, "phool patti"** (flower and leaf; part 7). Every province has its own style: watercolour and mirrors, precise sticker work, carved wood with camel-bone inlay. One painter's truck flowers later went onto a pair of Nike sneakers.
- **Kazuo Oga** (episode 5). The background painter behind the Studio Ghibli look.
- **Nianhua** (Chinese New Year prints). Visual puns where an object stands for a word that sounds alike: a fish reads as surplus, a bat as fortune. He calls this "a new way to think".
- **Schizocollage**, close to corecore and weirdcore (credit @creamydreamy).
- **Gen X Softclub**, his quick recipe:
  - backgrounds of 90s to 2000s architecture (glass buildings, metros, tunnels);
  - a subject in motion blur or out of focus;
  - everything pushed into blue and cyan so nothing feels dramatic;
  - grain and digital artefacts, as if found in an archive;
  - only plain sans-serifs (Helvetica, Arial, Univers) that "just exist".
- **Webcore.** Late-90s homepages built on GeoCities, Angelfire and Tripod: tiled backgrounds, Times New Roman, chunky buttons, autoplaying MIDI, visitor counters, starfields. He says to look on the Wayback Machine and GeoCities galleries, not on Pinterest.
- **Cyber sigilism, PS2-era UI, machine-learning bounding boxes** (Fake it part 4). Rave and post-human signals, low-resolution textures and clumsy interfaces, detection boxes that frame the composition.
- **Late-90s and early-2000s print ads**, especially tech ads (Sony, Nokia). "One object in focus" on a plain background with a large, obvious headline.
- **Japanese graphic design.** Study Tadanori Yokoo and Shigeo Fukuda.
- **Film title sequences** as an art form. Watch The Taste of Tea, Monster, Texhnolyze.
- **Hiromix** (photography). His reel is region-locked; the name only.
- **Childish art.** The Blaue Reiter almanac of 1912 (Kandinsky, Franz Marc).
- **Ugly on purpose:** "if you can make something ugly work, by design, you've created culture."
- **Imperfections make the project:** ink bleed and paper texture for print, grain for video, dust for 3D; dithering for all of them.

## His quick graphic-design moves ("Fake it till you make it", parts 1 to 5)

1. Add micrographics: coordinates, old packaging info, small brand hints. It adds depth and "the illusion of meaning".
2. Mix fonts: a thin serif with a bold sans.
3. Tear one photo into pieces and spread them across the canvas.
4. Use cutouts and borders aggressively to frame the subject, with a colour complementary to the image.
5. Leave lots of empty space, with tiny type that makes people squint.
6. Avoid ready-made fonts: letters made from real objects, or hand-drawn ("the uglier the better") next to clean text.
7. Overlay grids on organic shapes and materials.
8. Pixelate, or turn type and images into ASCII.
9. Repeat words, shapes or one cut-out image, for chaos or for balance.
10. One object in focus on a plain background, with a large serif headline stating something obvious.
11. From old rave magazines: rough paper-like cuts through an image with other images behind, or half the page one striking image and half simple text with a small drawing.

## What this teaches us (Claude's note, not a decision)

- **Find the source, not the repost.** Old rave zines, flyers, manuals and print ads on the Internet Archive are where today's looks came from; nobody else's board is built from them. This fits the cover and flyer jobs and his electronic lane's rave roots.
- **Name the look and its origin.** Same lesson as Waviboy (R-006): a named aesthetic plus its history gives a brief more depth than adjectives.
- **The talking-reel format is learnable:** a reference fills the frame, he is cut out in front, the picture changes on every phrase, a numbered series title with its own type, and one-word captions. If Amir ever talks about his references or process on camera ("post your working"), this is a ready shape.
- **A series with numbered volumes** turns single tips into something people follow.
