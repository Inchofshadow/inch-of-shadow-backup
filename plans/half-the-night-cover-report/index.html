The style: 90s noir

Noir is the name of this cover's world: 90s noir animation, also called dark deco. It is the look of Batman: The Animated Series, painted on black paper.

  • Flat shapes, few inks: warm black, ivory, one red. Plum came in as your 30%.
  • One big disc: the moon is the only hot point.
  • Silhouettes, almost no detail, hard shadows, low angles.
  • Texture from print, not from detail: grain, Xerox, a torn paper edge.

In prompts, the words that kept noir alive were "1990s dark deco animation style", "painted on black paper" and "hard black shadows". The words that killed it were "minimal" and "flat graphic poster". Every folder of this cover lives under artwork/edits/htn-noir/, and the references are tagged 90s-noir in the Shadow Archive, so the next noir release starts from here.

How you decide

Reading back six days of picks, the same patterns show up every time. They are what made this fast once the tools were right.

  • By eye, with numbers. You answer "13", "2 + shadow", "A". Vague questions slow you down; real pictures side by side get an answer in seconds.
  • You kill directions fast and say why. The searchlight moon: "the idea is good, the execution is bad, you lost the Batman language." One clear sentence saved a day.
  • You keep what works and say "as is". Grain on the letters: chosen, keep as is, even with the colour shift. You don't reopen settled things.
  • Rules you already had, made visible. 60/30/10 colours, red only on the moon, text never moves, never on the moon. You stated them once and they held to the end.
  • References carry the feeling. Batman: The Animated Series, Looney Tunes' Marvin, Dragon Ball's Frieza earth. Each one pointed at something concrete: flat silhouettes, the pink earth, the movement.
  • Heart over mind, after checking the mind. Switzer read better on paper. The thumbnail test showed no real difference, so Barlow won on feeling, with a reason behind it.
  • Subtle is invisible to you. Versions within 20% of each other got "I don't see too much change." Bold differences got decisions.
  • You push back when something is off, and you're often right. The red cut line looked wrong to you before I saw it. The pink moonlight was wrong, and you asked about it.
  • The last 10% came from asking "what do you honestly think?" That question found the weak bottom half, and the moonlight came out of it.

Local + Canva

What worked

  • AI only for the base, then local. About 20 drafts total. Every change after that cost nothing and could be undone.
  • Live pages, not render rounds. Once the cover lived as layers, a change took 0.1 s instead of a new image round.
  • One working tab. The picks side page, newest row on top, tap to zoom and slide, Undo. You stopped getting lost between editor and sheets.
  • Stages, one at a time. Elements, colours, text, effects, light. Each one closed before the next opened.
  • Everything kept and numbered. 50 keeps plus an archive. Nothing deleted, every decision traceable.
  • Decisions written down as they happened. The cover notes file held every pick, so nothing was re-argued.
  • Asking for an honest opinion. The best step came from "what do you honestly think?", not from more options.
  • Physics as a design tool. The moonlight looked right because it was calculated, not painted.

What cost us time

  • Too many tools at once. The 26-effect wall and the per-tree tools were "too complex, more decisions for nothing". Both were cut back.
  • Versions too close together. A whole round of 5-20% tweaks got "I don't see the change".
  • Numbers shifting. Archiving keeps renumbered them twice, and you had to re-find your picks.
  • Font licences checked late. Avenir looked final until its price ($97.99 a style) showed up. Barlow saved it.
  • Resolution checked late. The art is 1024 px. Enlarging to 3000 at the very end worked, but it should be planned from day one.
  • Bugs found by you, not me. The circle in the circle, the ghost owl, the reappearing stars, the grain that silently failed at 3000 px.
  • Shared checkout. Another session's history rewrite dropped the tournament code once, and the server was restarted by other chats.
  • No second opinion. Fable's week ran out before the review.

Next time: the workflow

We planned this one stage by stage, and it held. The next cover should start from this plan, while every stage stays a module you can skip, repeat or reorder.

Before any picture

  1. Check the deadline and size first. Store size (3000 px minimum), release date, and when the cover must be final.
  2. Pick from free fonts first. Start the font list from what we own and what is free (OFL). Paid fonts only if one wins clearly, and with the price shown up front.
  3. Render the base at 2048 px or more, or plan the enlargement from the start.

The modules

  1. Draft wide with AI: 3 to 6 short prompts, clearly different.
  2. Freeze one base and split it into layers.
  3. Elements, colours, text, effects, light, as separate stages. Skip any stage that the art doesn't need.
  4. Pick on the side page, with bold versions only (different at phone size).
  5. Ask "what do you honestly think?" before calling it final. It found the weak spot here.
  6. Second opinion (Fable) on the near-final, booked before the weekly limit runs out.
  7. Export from the page at full size, then Canva only for small touches.

Tool fixes to make

  • Make the Layout page work for any release, not only Half the Night. Today it is built around this one picture.
  • Give keeps fixed IDs that never shift when others are archived.
  • Add an Export button on the page (3000 and 4000 px), so no hand-run scripts are needed.
  • Have the picks side page update itself from the keeps, so it never falls behind your editor.
  • Run my own check before showing you anything: a zoom at 100% plus the server log, every time.

When you are in the picture

The same pipeline works for covers with you in them. Only the first module changes, and a few rules get stricter.

What stays the same

Freeze a base, split it into layers, then elements, colours, text, effects and light, live and local. The picks page and the way you decide don't change.

What changes

  • The base is a real photo, not an AI draft. Start from the shoot catalogue (204 tagged edits) and the photos you like.
  • Never AI on your face. No AI change to any of your photos without your go, per image. The subject cutout has to be made locally.
  • The layers are different: you, the background, the light, the text. Effects go on the background by default; anything on you is a choice you make on purpose.
  • No text over your face. Before placing type, check where your face and head are in each version.
  • Light gets harder. The moonlight trick works on a flat hill. On a face it must follow real shape, so relighting you stays subtle and is checked at 100% zoom.
  • Credits. The photographer is credited on Instagram only.

To build when we get there

  • A local "split the photo" step: you, background, and the light direction read from the photo.
  • A face-safe zone on the Layout page that text and effects stay out of.

Upgrading the tools: one studio

Today the Shadow Archive, Effects, Fonts and Imagine are four separate pages. On this cover we used them only partly, because opening them meant leaving the work. The upgrade is to bring them to the work.

Bring the libraries into the Layout page

  • A studio drawer on the side: references from the archive, effects with examples, fonts with licence and price, and Imagine elements. Drag one in, or apply it, without leaving the page.
  • One tag list across all four, so one search finds everything for a feeling. For example 90s-noir, dark-deco, moon, owl, half-the-night.

Record as we go, not after

  • Effects: four new ones from this cover, each with a before/after from Half the Night. Calculated red moonlight, grain + shadow on letters, cut line strength, ink corners.
  • Fonts: Barlow Condensed (chosen), Switzer Black, Fraunces Black, CAT Neuzeit Schatten, with licences.
  • Shadow Archive: your three noir references (the BTAS title card, the villain skyline poster, the comic skyline with the moon) are only in a build folder now. They belong in the archive, tagged with the reasons you gave: flat black silhouettes, red sky, "the Batman language".
  • Imagine: the cover's elements as a reusable set for this release. The red moon with the owl, the trees, the new tree, the crescent, the stars. Plus the prompt that worked (V1a / V1c) as a "90s noir" recipe, so the next AI drafts start from it.

The AI budget: spend more, earlier

We spent about $1 to 2 on AI for the whole cover. That was too careful. Drafts are cheap, and the weakest part of the final file is the one AI decided: the base painting is only 1024 px.

  • Why 1024 px: our render script is set to 1024 × 1024 for every render. That is why the cover had to be enlarged 3× at the end, and why the owl's edges show steps at 100% zoom.
  • Explore wider: 20 to 30 medium drafts (about $0.05 each, so $1 to 1.50) in the first hour, not spread over several days.
  • Re-render the finalists at high quality (about $0.21 each) and at the largest size the model offers, before freezing one. A new render is a new painting, so quality has to be chosen before the base is frozen, not after.
  • Or upscale the chosen base with AI once it is picked. That is an AI change to your image, so it needs your go first.
  • Fix to make: give the render script a size option (today it only does 1024). Check which sizes the model allows before the next cover.

Why it took six days, and how to stop that

Most of the six days went into building tools, not into deciding. About 30 of the ~90 commits record cover decisions; the rest built or fixed the pages. That was worth it once. It should not happen again.

  • The tools now exist. Elements, colours, effects per element, text with grain and shadow, calculated light, picks page, export. The next cover starts on day one with all of them.
  • One job before the next cover: make the Layout page work for any release and any base picture. Do it as its own ticket, not during a cover.
  • Tool freeze during a cover: only fixes. A new feature waits on a list unless it blocks a decision you are making that day.
  • Time box per stage: drafts and base on day one, stages on day two, finish and export on day three.

Real photos and photoreal AI images

This cover was easy to work in layers because it is flat ink: a few colours, hard edges, no gradients. A real photo or a photoreal AI image changes what each module can do.

A real photo (you in it)

  • Layers need a real cutout: you, hair edges, background. Local only, never AI on your face.
  • Recolouring becomes grading: we can't repaint a sky like a flat ink. Colour moves become tints, curves and the palette ramps.
  • Light is already there. Any added light (like our moonlight) must match the real light direction in the photo, or it looks fake.
  • Resolution is an upside: camera files are big, so no enlarging.
  • Rules: no text over your face, retouch only with your go per image, photographer credited on Instagram.

A photoreal AI image

  • Masks get harder: soft gradients and fine detail, so our colour-by-mask painting won't work cleanly. It needs proper segmentation.
  • AI mistakes hide in detail: hands, text, fake stamps, warped edges. Check every finalist at 100% zoom.
  • Render at high quality and full size from the start, since the detail is the point.
  • Store rules: distributors and stores may ask whether artwork is AI-made. We haven't checked Symphonic's rule yet. That applies to this cover too, since its base is AI.

Squeeze the lemon: the leftovers build the world

A cover round leaves dozens of good images that didn't win. They are not waste. Put in the cover's style, they make the release a world instead of one picture. On this cover, the early big-moon painting (1c) and the low-angle trees (B) came back in one quick pass, local, no AI.

New last step for every cover

  1. I suggest a leftovers check once the cover is final: the strongest unused drafts, side by side.
  2. You pick fast, like today: yes, no, or "add it to the world".
  3. I put the picks in the cover's style with a local script, and they go into the campaign folder.

Where leftovers can live

  • Back cover and inner art (vinyl, Bandcamp extra images).
  • Artist profile images on streaming platforms, so your profile looks like the release.
  • Spotify Canvas, reels, YouTube visuals made from a second composition, not the cover again.
  • Countdown and announcement posts, stories, press kit, merch, posters.
  • The next release: a leftover can open the next chapter of the same world.

What this teaches our LLM system

The cover is one release. What the system learned from it should make every next chat faster and better, without you repeating yourself. Here is what moves from this chat into the system, and what should be built.

Already saved

  • Memory notes: the cover method (layered, live, modular), "see it early, decide it late" for finishing tools, the leftovers check as the last step, and "versions must differ at a glance".
  • The decision log: every pick and its reason in the cover notes file. Long chats get compressed, and that file kept the details safe when they did.
  • Handover + tickets: what is open sits on the board, not in a chat you have to find again.

To build

  • A cover skill. Today the method lives in a report and a memory note. As a skill, any chat that starts a cover loads it on its own: the day-one checks (deadline, store size, free fonts, base resolution), the stages, the picks page, the leftovers step.
  • A structured pick log. Every round saved as data: what was shown, what you picked, your words. Over a few releases that becomes a real record of your eye, so my suggestions start closer to your taste. Taste rules still only come from 📌 questions after your read-back.
  • Self-check before showing. You found the circle in the circle, the ghost owl and the missing grain before I did. A fixed check (100% zoom, the server log, a before/after diff) runs on every picture before it reaches you.
  • Model roles, planned per week. Opus builds, Fable reviews near-final work. This week Fable ran out before the review, so the second-opinion slot gets booked early in the week for anything with a deadline.
  • Tools the model can drive. The Canva upload worked through its connector. Editors with an MCP server (PhotoCraft, once it is out of alpha) would let me apply edits by command instead of building pages for them.
  • Ask for the honest opinion as a standard step. The best move on this cover came from "what do you honestly think?". It becomes a fixed stage before final, not something you have to ask for.
